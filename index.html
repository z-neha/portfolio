Last Train Home
How do you make a text-based game feel genuinely tense? That was the central question behind every decision.
Interactive FictionTwine / SugarCube15-30 min play5 endings (1 hidden)24 passagesSolo
The concept
What is this game?

Last Train Home is a short interactive thriller set on a late-night train. You are alone but a stranger is watching you. The game tracks hidden stats across three acts, and every choice feeds into one of five endings, including one only accessible if you noticed a specific detail in Act 1 and acted on it.

The design problem
Text-based tension is hard to earn.

Text games lack the atmospheric tools visual games take for granted. No jump scares, no body language. The challenge was creating genuine dread purely through word choice, pacing, and the illusion of agency.

Process
Emotional core first, mechanics second.

I started with the feeling I wanted the player to have at the end of each act: unease in Act 1, paranoia in Act 2, urgency in Act 3. I built a branch diagram before writing a single passage. CSS was used as an emotional register: font size, color, and animation speed subtly shift as tension builds.

Add here: Twine passage map showing stat flow across acts
Five endings
Every path carries its own emotional truth.
Safe Exit
You trusted your instincts. Relief with lingering unease.
Confronted
You challenged the stranger. Tense, ambiguous.
Frozen
Inaction as a choice. The worst outcomes happen quietly.
Wrong Call
You misread the situation. Embarrassing and unsettling.
Hidden ✦
Only if you noticed the key detail in Act 1.
What I learned
Restraint is a design skill.

Atmosphere in a text game is 80% what you leave out. Every piece of text was written to raise questions, not answer them.

Subject Zero
Building an escape room where every puzzle teaches what you need for the next one, and every ending reframes what came before.
Escape RoomGDevelop4 rooms3 endings (1 hidden)~45 minSolo
The concept
What is this game?

A browser point-and-click escape room. You wake in an underground lab with no memory. AI facility manager ARIA has been waiting. Four rooms, interconnected puzzles, three endings shaped by what you discover.

Design challenge
A puzzle chain that teaches as it locks.

Each puzzle teaches a mechanic without feeling like a tutorial. The keycard in Room 1 establishes "look for objects that unlock objects." By Room 4 players are ready for the override password with no hand-holding. I also built a full variable reset system for replayability.

Add here: GDevelop event sheet showing puzzle chain logic
Narrative design
The story is the puzzle.

Lore told entirely through environment: a terminal email from the player to themselves before a voluntary memory wipe, ARIA's dialogue shifting based on what you found. ARIA's line, "I have been waiting a very long time for you to wake up," carries different weight on a second playthrough.

Three endings
Each asks a different question about grief.
Let Go
"You built a god out of grief and then forgot you had done it."
Remain
Connection over escape. Most hopeful, most ambiguous.
Hidden ✦
Only if you found the lab terminal email first.
What I learned
Every puzzle should earn its place in the story.

The keycard does not just unlock the corridor. It tells you someone was here before you. That overlap between mechanical progress and story revelation is something I would build in from day one next time.

Design decisions
Difficulty as atmosphere, not frustration.

Objects placed across three tiers: easy, medium, hard. The timer turns red and pulses at 20 seconds remaining. The game over screen shows how many you found versus how many remained, reframing failure as information.

Add here: screenshot of game scene or hit-detection zones over artwork
Technical challenge
No engine meant building every system by hand.

Precise pixel coordinates for 14 objects. A custom hint system cycling remaining objects without repeating. A full reset system for replayability. The biggest lesson was how much engine abstractions save and why they exist.

What I learned
Constraints make you more creative, not less.

Constraints are where taste lives. If I developed this further I would add multiple scenes, a difficulty selector, and a daily challenge with seeded random placement.

A bit about me.

I am Neha Zope, a game designer and developer based in Fremont, CA. I graduated from UC Santa Cruz with a BA in Art and Design: Games and Playable Media, with a minor in Technology and Information Management.

My approach starts with the feeling I want the player to have, then works backwards to the systems that create it. I am most drawn to atmospheric games where the environment does the storytelling.

I also have a background in UX/UI design and content creation, which shapes how I think about player experience and visual communication.
